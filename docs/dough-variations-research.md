# Dough variations — research notes

Compiled 2026-10-11 from the sources listed at the end of each section. These notes are the
evidence behind `PLAN-doughs.md`; the plan cites them as R1 to R4. Percentages are baker's
percentages of **total flour** (starter flour included) unless stated. Temperatures are
conventional-oven °C unless "fan" is stated. Figures marked _(forum)_ or _(unsourced)_ come
from home-baker threads or popular blogs rather than a serious baking source.

Abbreviations: KA = King Arthur Baking, PL = The Perfect Loaf, TSJ = The Sourdough Journey,
TFL = The Fresh Loaf forum, BBGA = Bread Bakers Guild of America, SD = sourdough, Y = yeasted.

## R1 · Roll and bun sizes

The current `roll` preset at 70 g is a **dinner roll** by every source read. A standard
4-inch (10 cm) burger bun is **100 g** of dough in home recipes; sourdough bun recipes run
**120–135 g**; industrial buns are 60–70 g for the same diameter and far airier.

Rules the plan relies on:

1. **Temperature follows the dough, not the piece size.** Crusty lean rolls: 220–230 °C
   with steam. Soft, enriched or sweet rolls: "a longer amount of time at a lower
   temperature", 175–190 °C (160–170 fan), no steam (KA yeast guide; ChainBaker). Two-stage
   bakes appear once pieces reach ~90–135 g (PL 220→175 °C hot dog buns, 230→220 °C buns).
2. **Time scales slowly with weight.** From one dough at 190 °C: 33 g and 66 g buns 12–15
   min, 100 g 15–18 min (KA Beautiful Burger Buns). Free-standing 30–100 g pieces at
   190–205 °C: 12–18 min; 100–150 g: 20–25 min. Pan-packed, touching rolls take 5–10 min
   longer. The sourdough recipes read ran 5–10 min longer than yeasted at equal weight.
3. **Diameter.** The only explicit ladder is Stella Culinary's brioche in ring moulds:
   55 g → 3 in (7.6 cm), 100 g → 4 in (10 cm), 155 g → 4.5 in (11.4 cm). Derived rule of
   thumb, free-form: grams ≈ (diameter in cm)². Commercial airy buns run 40–50 % under this.
4. **Doneness.** 88–93 °C internal for soft enriched rolls (CIA 88, ChainBaker ~92, KA 93);
   93–97 °C for lean (PL 95–97).
5. Nobody publishes a "grams per inch" rule or a commercial scaling chart beyond IRCA's
   60 g industrial bun. The BAKERpedia guide gives sizes (burger 4–5 in, slider 2–3 in) and
   times (10–15 min buns, 20–25 min cluster rolls), not weights.

### Fermentation effects

- **Sugar is the one quantified effect.** ChainBaker (yeasted, 60 % hydration): final proof
  45 min at 3 %, 60 at 5 %, 75 at 10 %, 90 at 20 % — "with each increase the dough took an
  extra 15 minutes"; "significant decrease… from around 10 %, even 5 % will slow it"; ≤3 %
  may help a little. Gisslen: at ≥12 % sugar use osmotolerant yeast or ×1.3 yeast, proof
  ≤27 °C, rich doughs are "generally underproofed… about three-quarters proof".
  Timmermans et al., Foods 2022 (yeasted, 30 °C, 3 h): total CO₂ 108 mL at 0 % sucrose
  (sugar ran out), 204 at 7 %, 158 at 14 %, 94 at 21 %. Sourdough: PL raised PFF from 7.25
  to 8.5 % to keep a 7 %-honey pain de mie at ~4 h bulk; "high sugar levels can slow natural
  fermentation"; sweet levains (20 % sugar inside the levain) are used to reduce sourness.
- **Fat.** No measured slowing at 10 % (ChainBaker: four fats at 10 % behaved identically).
  High butter makes a firm, cool dough that ferments slowly; Modernist and Wordloaf say
  extended, warmer proofing (26–29 °C) is often necessary. "Fat coats the yeast" is
  folklore.
- **Egg.** No source says eggs slow fermentation; the only test (ChainBaker) found egg and
  yolk doughs proofed faster. "Eggs slow fermentation" is folklore.
- **Milk.** Minor; no timing numbers anywhere.
- **No source gives an "x % longer than lean" multiplier**; the authors compensate with a
  higher PFF and a sweet levain. Observed bulks at 23–27 °C: 3.5–6 h for buns, rolls,
  shokupan and pain de mie at PFF 8.5–14; ~10 h at PFF 5.
- The plan's bulk heuristic, derived from the ChainBaker series and capped: factor
  = 1 + 0.05 × max(0, sugar % − 3), + 0.1 when butter + oil ≥ 20 %, clamped to 1–2. It is
  a guide, editable per dough, and is shown as such.

### When and how each enrichment goes in

- **Egg and milk: at the mix, always.** Gisslen, Modernist, ASB, PL and KA all put them in
  with the liquid; no source adds egg or milk to an already-developed dough, and their
  water has to be inside the hydration from the start.
- **Sugar: at the mix** (half at the start and half after 6 min is as late as anyone goes).
  Adding sugar during bulk is undocumented; it loosens the dough.
- **Butter.** Up to ~10 % can go in at the start (ChainBaker; KA's milk bread adds 18 %
  melted with everything). Above that, after gluten development, in pieces: KA Pro "to full
  gluten development, then pliable butter in chunks until the dough sheets out"; PL
  room-temperature pats one at a time over 5–8 min; Sourdough Brandon 1 Tbsp every ~30 s then
  7–10 min to windowpane, "by hand allow ~5 min more"; Bake with Jack by hand: dimple in
  thirds and fold until absorbed. Cold butter by hand, soft in a mixer. Keep the dough under
  26–28 °C. Everyone agrees ≥40 % must be late.
- **Bassinage** is a water technique (hold back 5–15 % water, add at ~70 % development),
  "not suited to tin loaves and rolls" (Busby's). The late-butter method is analogous.
- **Verdict on "mix one lean dough, split it, enrich the portion":**
  - **Butter: legitimate and documented.** It is the classic brioche sequence, and fold-in
    during bulk exists: ChainBaker's hand-mixed brioche works 30 % cold butter in during
    15 min of stretch-and-folds after a 10 min knead; Pantry Mama adds 20 % grated frozen
    butter during fold sets 2 and 3 of 4. Costs: the dough goes "slimy and stringy" until
    rests and folds bring it back (TFL), distribution is less even, the portion rises
    slower and lower, and it must stay cool.
  - **Oil ≤10 %: fine** (at the start or folded in; above 10 % it reads as extra hydration).
  - **Sugar: weakly supported**, ≤5 % plausible, untested.
  - **Egg and milk: not supported by any source.** They belong in the mix water.
  - So: a split is sound for butter and oil only; egg, milk, sugar and tangzhong variants
    need their own mix.

### Shaping, proofing, baking

- **Oven:** enriched breads bake at 150–205 °C versus 220–250 °C for lean. Buns 190 °C
  15–25 min (KA, Gisslen, Brandon, ChainBaker brioche 190 °C no fan); ChainBaker buns and
  milk bread 160 °C fan 25–35 min; milk bread and pain de mie 175 °C 30–40 min lidded (PL, KA);
  Wordloaf 205 °C 10 min → 165 °C 40–45 min. Egg and sugar brown faster, so "enriched doughs
  should not be baked at very high temperatures"; "the more sugar the lower the baking
  temperature" (ChainBaker).
- **Internal temperature — sources disagree:** 88 °C (ATK, KA) vs 93–95 °C (PL) vs ~99 °C
  (Wordloaf). ATK's test: ±3 °C made no crumb difference; crust colour was the real
  difference. Plan default: 88–93 °C enriched, 96–99 °C lean, "bake to colour".
- **Wash:** whole egg + a splash of milk or water → very golden and shiny; cream → medium
  golden, shiny; milk → medium golden, dull; butter → pale. None needed under a Pullman lid.
- **Steam — sources disagree:** none (Wordloaf: sugar delays gelatinisation; PL, KA,
  ChainBaker) vs less than lean (Gisslen) vs plenty (Bread Code, Bake with Jack). Plan
  default: none.
- **Proof extent:** three-quarters (Gisslen; over-proofed units collapse) to doubled (buns:
  Brandon, ChainBaker); pan loaves to just below the rim; proof at 27 °C (29 °C sourdough).
  Many retard after bulk because cold butter shapes cleanly.
- **Gluten:** a mixer is preferred ("given the added egg, butter and sugar" — PL); hand
  mixing works with slap-and-fold but can melt the butter. Mixer totals 15–20 min; hand
  25–30 min. Strong flour. DDT 24–26 °C. Folds in sourdough recipes: PL 3 sets (rolls, pain
  de mie), 2 sets (shokupan); mixer-only recipes do none. Bulk targets +40 % to doubled.

### Tangzhong and yudane

Cook 5–10 % of the flour with liquid at 1:5 (tangzhong, to ~65 °C) or 1:1 with boiling
liquid (yudane); PL 4–8 %, ChainBaker 4–10 %. Take the flour and liquid out of the recipe
totals, not on top, then raise the total hydration to ~75 % because the gelatinised liquid
does not loosen the dough (KA; a roux without extra liquid "rises much less"). Softer,
moister, keeps days longer; not for crusty breads; the two methods differ negligibly. No
fermentation effect claimed.

### Low-level fat in a lean or sandwich dough

2–3 % butter or oil is typical for sandwich loaves: softer crumb and crust, more extensible,
keeps a day or two longer, process otherwise unchanged, added at the mix (TFL). Wordloaf:
≤5 % fat behaves like water (swap 1:1); >5 % wants stronger flour or longer mixing; ≥10 % to
taste it. ChainBaker: butter adds ~1 % hydration; oil above 10 % behaves as extra hydration.

### Folklore and disagreements, for the record

"Fat coats the yeast" and "eggs slow fermentation" are not supported. Scalding milk is
legacy advice. The sugar threshold where slowing becomes material is 5–10 % (ChainBaker),
12 % (Gisslen), 10–30 % (Lesaffre's osmotolerant yeast). Butter timing: classic
"after full gluten" versus "fine at the start up to ~10 %". Internal temperature 88 vs 95 vs
99 °C. Steam none vs some vs plenty. Milk conversion 1:1 vs +10–14 %.

### Hydration

- **Dry absorbent inclusions pull water** (seeds, grains, nuts, unsoaked fruit). Soak:
  KA grain soaker at 20 % of flour with equal-weight water, 2 h to overnight, pour off the
  excess; PL flax/sesame 1:1 in boiling water ~5 h (sunflower not soaked); Hamelman flax
  1:3, 16 h. ChainBaker: boiling water over nuts/raisins/millet, cool, drain well.
- **Is soaker water part of hydration? Sources disagree.** PL counts it. The BBGA
  convention (and Hamelman's formula layout, and Wordloaf) does not: the soaker is its own
  build, "hydration neutral", its water listed but outside the hydration figure, and soaked
  grains are not flour. The plan follows BBGA: soaker water is a separate ingredient line.
- **Wet inclusions**: PL adds 8 % raw jalapeño and 23 % rinsed-and-dried olives at 75 %
  with no water change; Tartine olive "water can be reduced by up to 5 %"; Flourist "lower
  the water slightly". Pat olives and pickled peppers dry. Roast potato/vegetables first.
- **Porridge** slackens the dough late in bulk → −5 % water (PL).
- **Purées**: count only their water fraction toward hydration (Myhrvold).
- **Fatty inclusions (cheese)**: no source reduces water. Oil pools under the loaf and can
  scorch the base → parchment (PL, TSJ, Breadtopia).
- The only quantitative holdback anyone states: keep back ~5 % of the water (Wordloaf; PL's
  reserved "water 2", 5–10 %) and add it only if the dough can take it.

### Fermentation and salt

- Salty, fatty, garlic and cinnamon inclusions slow fermentation (TSJ) — asserted, never
  measured. PL does **not** lengthen bulk for 25 % cheddar (3.5 h at 23–24 °C, same as
  plain) nor for 23 % olives. Dried fruit: KA says sugar slows it, TSJ says it speeds it
  slightly; unquantified. Breadtopia: heavy loads limit the rise (~33 %); lamination
  de-gasses, so bulk a little less and proof longer.
- Salt: PL keeps 1.8 % with 25 % cheddar and with rinsed olives; Brød & Taylor keeps 2 %
  with 28 % cheddar. Wordloaf's rule: add salt for half the dry inclusion weight at the base
  percentage (unsalted seeds/nuts), skip it for fruit and chocolate, subtract the same for
  salty inclusions (olives → ~1.6 %). The plan surfaces this as an info note, not a change.
- Pickled-jalapeño brine: acetic acid inhibits yeast (patent literature); no baking source
  quantifies it. KA simply drains the peppers.

### Baking and shaping

- PL bakes inclusion loaves exactly like plain (230 °C, 20 min steamed + 30 min) to 95 °C
  internal (seeded 97 °C); drop ~15 °C for the last stretch if colouring fast. Brød & Taylor
  232 °C lidded 20 min → 218 °C 18–20 min. Internal targets across sources: 93–97 °C.
  High-moisture inclusions may need a longer bake (Flourist). Nobody claims cheese needs a
  higher internal temperature.
- Keep cheese inside; surface cheese for a full 45 min bake burns (readers' loaves
  "scorched on the bottom"). Parchment under the loaf. Bran on the outside absorbs oil.
- Sugary inclusions (fruit, chocolate): tuck any piece poking out back in or it chars;
  soaked raisins burn less; watch the last 15 min.
- Shaping: pre-shape gently, inclusion dough tears; line the basket (cranberries bleed);
  Breadtopia skips the pre-shape because inclusions reduce surface tension.

### Splitting one batch (plain half + inclusion half)

Not documented by any professional source reached; common and endorsed home practice on
TFL: divide **at the first fold** by weight and fold the inclusions into one portion, or
divide right after mixing and work the cheese in; track the two bulks separately. Pitfalls
named: the inclusion half may ferment a little slower (asserted); hydration cannot be
corrected after gluten development; laminating after bulk de-gasses; piece weights come out
heavier than planned if the add-ins are not scaled into the piece weight. A bakery-scale
thread concluded two separate doughs are simpler once a dry mix changes the hydration.

### Recording the formula

- Every serious source lists inclusions as **lines in the total formula, as % of total
  flour**, like water or salt. Soakers and porridges are their own builds (flax 100 %,
  water 300 %; porridge grain 100 %, water 200 %, salt 2 %), with the inclusion line
  footnoted to the build (BBGA BreadLines 2016 "The Migration"; Hamelman / KA Pro).
- **Total dough weight includes the inclusions.** PL's worked example sizes
  flour × (1 + 0.76 + 0.019 + 0.008 + 0.15) = 1 800 g for 15 % walnuts; his
  jalapeño-cheddar is 2 × 900 g pieces _including_ 277 g of cheese and pepper. BBGA's
  total dough weight includes porridge and fruit. The alternative (Wordloaf): inclusions
  compact the crumb, so reduce the surrounding dough by only 30–50 % of the inclusion
  weight rather than keeping the piece weight constant — test and adjust per tin.

### What changes with no sugar at all

1. Fermentation runs at the unsugared rate. In sourdough the lactic bacteria are no longer
   suppressed, so the bun can be tangier: use a young white-flour levain and do not
   over-proof (PL, Wordloaf).
2. Paler crust. Compensate with milk or 5–10 % milk powder and an egg-plus-milk wash, and
   bake to internal temperature (KA 88 °C, PL 95 °C), not to colour. No serious source
   raises the oven for a no-sugar dough; ChainBaker only lowers it as sugar rises.
3. Slightly firmer, drier crumb and faster staling (Sahin 2017). Compensate with a
   tangzhong (KA; PL rolls cook 7 % of the flour in 28 % milk), milk, 14–16 % butter, egg.
4. Volume goes up. 5. Blander, obviously not sweet. 6. Easier handling, stronger gluten.
5. Low sugar is normal: Hamelman soft rolls ≈6 %, KA sandwich bread 7 %, PL rolls 9 %, and
   KA: "most savoury yeast breads include no sugar at all". No serious source publishes a
   sugar-free milk bread; the published position is "reduce, keep a little", and the only
   losses named are colour and sweetness. A sugar-reduction review (Sahin 2019) lists the
   same roles and proposes sourdough-made mannitol as a replacer.

### For the planner

1. Default enriched doughs to no sugar; keep milk (or milk powder), butter 10–16 %, egg,
   tangzhong and an egg-plus-milk wash. Bake at the recipe temperature; stop at 88–96 °C.
2. No sugar → no sugar slowing in the bulk estimate; keep the fat term.
3. A bulk sweetener slows like its sugar-equivalent grams, scaled by the osmotic factor above;
   a high-intensity sweetener changes nothing.
4. Allulose is the only sweetener that browns and holds moisture (4–8 %, oven 10–15 °C
   lower), but it is not authorised in the EU or UK at the time of writing, so the planner
   leaves it off its list (decided 2026-10-11; the row above stays for the record). Xylitol
   at ≤5 % is the first choice that can be bought here (no browning; dogs). Erythritol
   blends are the weakest fit. Pure high-intensity sweeteners give sweetness only.
5. Label check: "cup-for-cup" products bulked with dextrose or maltodextrin are sugar by
   weight and will ferment and brown; erythritol-bulked ones will not. Show the bulking
   agent, not the headline sweetener.

Disagreements: onset of sugar slowing (3 % ChainBaker, ≤6 % Gélinas 2018, 10 % KA); allulose
and yeast; xylitol and volume. Folklore flagged: "sugar feeds the yeast so the bread rises
better"; "Swerve browns and caramelises"; "stevia makes yeast grow fastest".
